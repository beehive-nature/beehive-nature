# Law of the Sea - the estate's naval data language

Founder ruling, 2026-09-21. Drafted by the Cowork seat; published on the founder's word ("print publish").

## The rule

Every data flow on the estate is named in the language of the sea. Founder ruling, 2026-09-21: "hold hard to naval terms with data flow and management. Law of the sea."

The sea solved the estate's problems first. A ship moves valuable cargo between strangers, across waters nobody owns, with no central authority on board. Sailors answered that with manifests, logs, watches, shared-loss rules and a duty to rescue. Those are the same answers a serverless data network needs.

Three laws follow from the ruling:

- A data action gets the sea's word for it when one exists. The first is **manifest**: a file is added to the manifest before it sails to Autonomi.
- The word must be true to its meaning at sea. A term that only sounds naval is not used.
- A stranger must still understand the page. The naval word is the name; one plain sentence beside it says what happens.

## The glossary

A first draft of the working vocabulary, in the order a file travels. Only **manifest** is ruled; every other row is proposed.

| Term | At sea | On the estate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manifest | The list of everything the ship carries, declared before sailing | The list of files a person has chosen to send: name, size, fingerprint, status |
| Cargo | The goods being carried | The files themselves |
| Hold | The space below decks where cargo waits and rides | Local staging on the person's own device, before anything leaves |
| Bill of lading | The receipt a carrier gives for cargo taken aboard, stating what and for whom | The price quote and payment plan a person sees before paying |
| Clearance | Permission from the port to sail | The person's own authorization and spending ceiling |
| Pilot | The local expert who guides a ship through a harbor, never its owner | The bridge program: it prepares the passage and holds no keys |
| Make sail | Departure | The upload, once the wallet has paid |
| Log | The ship's written record, kept every watch, never erased | The receipt trail: address, chunks, amount paid, time |
| Port of call | Where cargo is delivered | The network a file lands on: Autonomi, Arweave, Hive, local |
| Landfall | First sight of the destination | The datamap address coming back |
| Sounding | Measuring the depth before moving forward | Verify by downloading and comparing the fingerprint |
| Watch | The crew on duty, in rotation, so the ship is never unattended | Seats on shift; checks that run on every change |
| Muster | Calling the roll to confirm everyone is accounted for | The registry count: every surface present and numbered |
| Colors | The flag that says whose ship this is | Signed identity on a message or commit |
| Ballast | Weight carried for stability, not for sale | Redundant copies kept so data survives |
| Jettison | Throwing cargo overboard to save the ship | Deleting what can be deleted; never possible for permanent public data |
| General average | All parties share the loss when cargo is jettisoned to save the voyage | Shared cost and shared loss across the hive, never one member alone |
| Salvage | Recovering a ship or cargo in peril | Resuming a paid but unfinished upload without paying twice |
| Dry dock | Out of the water for repair | A surface taken offline to be fixed |
| Shakedown | A new ship's first trial run | Tests against mocks before anything real is spent |
| Commission | A ship formally enters service | A surface goes live, proven at its public address |
| Sick bay | Where the corpsman treats the crew | Bug diagnosis and repair |

## Ten thousand years of sailors

People have crossed open water for more than ten thousand years, and nearly every rule the estate needs was written by them first. Dates below are approximate and written from general knowledge, not from sources opened for this draft; each row is to be checked against a source.

| When | What happened | What it gave us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| About 11,000 BCE | Obsidian from the island of Melos reaches mainland Greece, so people were already crossing open Aegean water | Proof that seafaring is older than farming, writing or the city |
| About 8000 BCE | The Pesse canoe, a dugout found in the Netherlands, the oldest known boat | The first surviving hull |
| About 5000 BCE | Reed boats sealed with bitumen sail the Persian Gulf | Trade by sea between early settlements |
| About 3000 BCE onward | Austronesian voyagers spread from Taiwan across the Pacific and Indian oceans | Wayfinding by stars, swells and birds, with no instruments |
| About 2500 BCE | The Khufu ship is buried beside the Great Pyramid | Plank-built ships made to last for eternity |
| About 1300 BCE | The Uluburun ship sinks off Turkey carrying copper, tin, glass and ivory from many lands | The oldest mixed cargo we can still inventory: a manifest on the seabed |
| About 1200 to 300 BCE | Phoenician traders work the whole Mediterranean and pass the alphabet along their routes | Trade networks that carried writing itself |
| About 800 BCE onward | The Rhodian sea law: cargo thrown overboard to save a ship is a loss shared by all | General average, still in force today |
| About 1000 CE | Norse sailors reach North America | Open-ocean crossing in the North Atlantic |
| About 1100 to 1300 CE | Polynesian voyagers settle Hawaii, Rapa Nui and Aotearoa | The largest settlement by sea in human history |
| 12th to 14th century | The Rolls of Oleron and the Consolato del Mare set out the rights and duties of masters and crews | Written law of the sea, carried from port to port |
| 1405 to 1433 | Zheng He's treasure fleets sail from China to East Africa | Fleet logistics on a scale Europe would not match for centuries |
| 1747 | James Lind's trial aboard HMS Salisbury shows citrus treats scurvy | Often called the first controlled clinical trial, run at sea by a ship's surgeon |
| 1760s | John Harrison's chronometer solves longitude | A ship can know where it is |
| 1898 | The United States Navy Hospital Corps is established on 17 June | The corpsman becomes a rating of his own |
| 1982 | The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea is signed | The high seas are held in common and belong to no one state |

## The founder in the line

The founder joined that line himself: he served as a United States Navy and Fleet Marine Force Hospital Corpsman. He joined the Navy at 17 and left for his first boot camp in 1995. That is what he shares publicly; units, stations and operations stay out of this document.

A Hospital Corpsman is the sailor who keeps the crew alive. A Fleet Marine Force corpsman does it ashore with the Marines, who call him "Doc" and call "Corpsman up" when someone is hit. The rating descends from the ship's surgeons and loblolly boys of the age of sail, the same tradition that produced Lind's scurvy trial.

The estate already runs on his two trades. The operating room gives it seats, a surgical team and S.O.A.P. notes for every bug. The ship gives it the manifest, the log and the watch. The corpsman is where the two meet: one person, trained to act alone, far from any hospital, responsible for everyone aboard.

That is the posture the estate asks of every seat. Treat what is in front of you, write it in the log, and never leave a shipmate behind.
