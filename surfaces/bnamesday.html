Why a name has a day

How Latvia chooses the names

A small commission at Latvia’s State Language Centre keeps the calendar. It meets every few years — 2018, 2022, 2025 — and weighs each proposed name: where it comes from, what it means, how it sounds in Latvian, and whether people really use it in one steady form.

There are two lists. The calendar is the one printed on kitchen walls: names. The long list adds more, so that almost everyone has a day.

The day for every other name

22 May is kept for “every unusual name no calendar has written down.” If your name isn’t here, that day is yours — the calendar says so itself.

29 February is the one day of the year with no names at all.

How Thailand names a day

In Thailand the weekday you were born on comes with a colour, a planet, and a posture of the Buddha — many people know theirs as surely as their birthday. Tradition turns the day at six in the morning, not midnight, and gives Wednesday night to Rahu, as a day of its own.

When a child is named, an old table called ทักษา (thaksa) sorts the Thai letters into eight groups, one per planet. Starting from your own day, each group falls into a house — honour, fortune, the elders who lift you. The eighth house, กาลกิณี, holds the letters to leave out.

Reading old family records

Latvia. Church books — baptisms, weddings, burials — are scanned and free to read at Raduraksti, the State Historical Archives’ reading room. Under the Russian Empire they were dated in the old calendar, 12 or 13 days behind ours. A child was often given a name from the days around the birth or the christening, so a name day can be a clue to a date, and a date a clue to a name. A clue — not proof.

Thailand. Family names only began in 1913, and the law keeps each one to a single family — so two people who share a Thai surname are almost certainly kin. Papers count years in the Buddhist Era.
