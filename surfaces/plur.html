peace
the first word

the word two peoples share

שלום · سلام

Shalom and salām are the same word. One Semitic root — S‑L‑M — meaning whole, entire, unbroken. Not the absence of war; the state of nothing being missing.

Two peoples who have agreed on almost nothing for a century greet each other every day, in their own languages, with the same word. Nobody had to negotiate that. It was already true before anyone drew a border.

And शान्तिः travelled the other way — Sanskrit śānti walked east with the sutras into Thai สันติภาพ and stayed in Hindi as शांति. Peace has been crossing frontiers longer than the frontiers have existed.

love
mīlestība ir karalis

one english word, many real ones

English has one word and makes it carry everything. Most of these don't. Turkish keeps aşk and sevgi apart — the fire and the tenderness — and using the wrong one is a real mistake. Persian عشق means a love that costs you something.

So "translation" is the wrong frame here. These aren't versions of an English word. They're distinctions English lost.

unity
the hive word

one, made into a thing

Sanskrit एकता is just eka — one — with a suffix that turns it into a noun. One‑ness. Hindi kept it unchanged for three thousand years. Turkish birlik does exactly the same trick with bir, its own word for one, from a completely unrelated family.

Two languages that share no ancestor reached for the same idea and built it the same way: take one, and make it something you can hold.

respect
the one that has to be given

respect has weight

Hebrew כבוד comes from a root meaning heavy. To respect someone is to treat them as having weight — as something that can't be waved aside. It's the same root behind glory.

And احترام travelled: Arabic into Turkic, and Tatar says хөрмәт to this day. A word for respect crossed a language border and was kept. That's the thing the word describes, happening to the word itself.

roses for the crossing

In Riga the flower shops never close, because you do not arrive empty‑handed. Red roses, always an odd number — in Latvia, even flowers are for the grave. The core is frozen; the garden is skin.
